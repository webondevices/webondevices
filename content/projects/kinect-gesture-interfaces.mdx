Walk up to a screen. Raise a hand. The cursor follows. Swipe and the content moves. Nobody touched anything, nobody downloaded an app, and nobody had to be shown how.

<Video youtube="Iad5BmZYUy8" title="Kinect 2 gesture tracking in the browser" />

## How it works

A Kinect 2 sensor tracks up to six people at once, 25 joints each, thirty times a second. I read that stream in Node.js, packaged every frame as JSON and pushed it over WebSockets. Any browser on the network could subscribe, including a Mac across the room that had no idea a Kinect was involved.

![What the sensor sees: depth and skeleton data](/posts/kinect1.png)

From there it's just web development. The browser gets hand positions, head position and body pose, and I turn those into gestures: point, grab, swipe, wave, step closer, walk away.

That last part matters. Because the interface is HTML, CSS and JavaScript, it can be anything a website can be. Video, 3D, live data, animation. And it runs full screen on whatever display you've got.

![The Kinect v2 sensor](/posts/kinect_v2.jpg)

## Where this belongs

**Shop windows.** A passer-by stops, and the display reacts. Wave to browse the new collection after hours, while the shop is closed.

**In-store kiosks.** Browse products, compare options, or check stock on a big screen without touching a grubby surface. Hygiene stopped being a niche concern a while ago.

**Museums and visitor centres.** Step into a zone and the exhibit wakes up. Lean in to zoom. Groups can explore together, because the sensor tracks several people at once.

**Events and launches.** A product reveal that responds to the crowd. A photo moment that tracks poses. Something people film and share.

**Receptions and waiting areas.** Wayfinding, check-in or a playful idle screen that notices when someone approaches.

## Today's version

The Kinect itself has been retired, but the idea is stronger than ever. Depth cameras, webcams with on-device pose estimation, and AI vision models now do the same job with smaller, cheaper hardware. I'd also add things that weren't practical in 2015: recognising what people are holding, estimating crowd size and dwell time, or adapting content to who's actually standing there.
